Returned Component Recovery

Overview

A returned component can be reused, remanufactured, or recycled. This project develops a decision model that selects the most profitable option for each component. When remanufacturing is preferred, it also selects the manufacturing route and the final condition to restore the component to.

How the decision model works

Inspection describes the component on two scales from 0 to 1, where 1 matches the market benchmark. Functional service condition measures how well it can perform its function. Marketable non-functional condition measures attributes such as surface finish and appearance that affect whether it can be sold. The model also accounts for the component's geometric complexity and how difficult it is to reach the damaged area.

The decision diagram shows the three linked choices. First, the model finds the best restoration target for each route by subtracting the cost of reaching it from its estimated selling price. It then picks the better of additive manufacturing (AM) and conventional manufacturing (CM). Finally, it compares that result with direct reuse, when the part already meets release requirements, and recycling, which recovers the value of the material.

What the model found

The recovery maps show which option is most profitable for each pair of incoming conditions. With low engineering difficulty, CM covers most of the remanufacturing region. At higher difficulty, the areas favoring AM, reuse, and recycling grow. The model also finds cases where improving one condition, partially improving both, or restoring only to the release thresholds gives the highest profit. The maps were recalculated at reduced resolution using the manuscript's baseline parameters.

Outputs

We analytically show that economically optimal remanufacturing need not fully restore a component to an as-new condition. The preferred target state may involve minimum restoration or full restoration, but also improvement mainly in one condition or intermediate improvement in both conditions. This choice depends on how the recovered value and restoration cost change with the extent of restoration, as well as the component’s condition upon return, geometric complexity, and accessibility difficulty. Through a numerical study, we show that direct reuse, remanufacturing, and recycling can each be optimal for components returned in different conditions. Remanufacturing using conventional manufacturing may work best when geometrical complexity and accessibility difficulty are low, while remanufacturing using additive manufacturing becomes more attractive when these factors make the conventional route costly. Compared to fixed rules that preselect the recovery strategy (e.g. reusing components that meet minimum condition requirements and recycling the rest), the proposed model increases net profit by more than 25% on average. Compared to the common practice of fully restoring components using conventional manufacturing, our model yields on average over 11 times the net profit.

The analytical rules reduced the average decision time for a single component from about 0.4 seconds to 0.005 seconds on the study hardware. The full decision maps took about 19 minutes to generate with those rules, compared with about 12 hours using the direct search. 80x faster decision making.
